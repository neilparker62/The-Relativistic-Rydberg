2. The Conventional Relativistic Formulation of Newton II

In standard textbook physics, when a particle with rest mass $m_0$ moves at a relativistic velocity $\vec{v}$, its linear momentum is defined as $\vec{p} = \gamma m_0 \vec{v}$. When applying Newton’s Second Law, standard physics defines force as the total time derivative of this momentum vector:

$$F = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{m_{0}\vec{v}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}\right)$$

Assuming one-dimensional linear motion where the net force $\vec{F}$ acts strictly parallel to the velocity vector $\vec{v}$, executing the calculus chain rule yields:

$$F = \frac{m_{0}}{\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)^{3/2}}\frac{dv}{dt} = \gamma^{3} m_{0}\frac{dv}{dt}$$

Feeding this standard differential equation to a mathematical engine under a constant, sustained linear force $F$ yields a hyperbolic trajectory:

$$v(t) = \frac{Ft}{\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+\frac{F^{2}t^{2}}{c^{2}}}}$$

As $t \to \infty$, $v(t)$ asymptotically approaches the speed of light $c$ without ever exceeding it. For an un-bound, free particle traveling linearly down a particle accelerator, this formulation is entirely accurate.

Because this constant force acts normal to the instantaneous path of motion, it does not continuously scale the forward linear velocity magnitude toward infinity. 6. Derivation of the Bohr Magneton

To map this geometric wave motion onto observable macroscopic quantum mechanics, we calculate the magnetic dipole moment (\(\mu_m\)) generated by this circulating charge. For a localized current loop carrying a charge \(-e\) traversing a closed path of major radius \(r = \overline{\lambda}_e\) at the light-speed current velocity \(c\), the effective electrical current \(I\) and loop area \(A\) are:

$$A = \pi r^2 = \pi \left(\frac{\hbar}{m_e c}\right)^2$$
$$I = \frac{-e}{T} = \frac{-e c}{2\pi r}$$
$$\mu_m = I \cdot A = \left(-\frac{e c}{2\pi r}\right)(\pi r^2) = -\frac{1}{2} e c \left(\frac{\hbar}{m_e c}\right) = -\frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = -\mu_B$$
